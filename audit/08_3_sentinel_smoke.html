Reviewer guide — listening to BP audio clips

This guide is for the human judge reviewing audio clips in the listening-checklist HTMLs (Stage 6/7 listening_checklist, Stage 8 calibration, Stage 8 after-fix QA).

Your profile

You are an A1 Brazilian Portuguese learner, native Polish speaker, fluent in English and German. The tips below lean on Polish, EN, and DE parallels where they're useful — you don't need any prior BP training to make these judgments.

What you're judging

You're judging whether the audio sounds like standard Brazilian Portuguese or like some other accent (English-leaning, European Portuguese, Spanish, French, etc.). You are not judging whether the voice timbre is pleasant, whether the recording is high-fidelity, or whether the speaker matches some idealized BP. The deck only needs each clip to clearly cue the BP pronunciation of the displayed word.

If a clip sounds clearly BP → OK. If it sounds clearly off (English / EP / Spanish / weird) → MISPRONOUNCED. If you can't tell, or it's borderline → UNCLEAR.

Six BP-specific phonetic features to listen for

These are the features that distinguish BP from English, EP, Spanish, and French in our voice catalog. Each one is a useful test.

1. Final -l vocalization (BP [w], not English [l])

BP turns syllable-final l into a u-like glide — similar to the Polish ł in łapa (which sounds like English "w"). English keeps a clear l. EP keeps a velarized but consonantal l.

animal[a-ni-MAU] (last syllable rhymes with English "wow")[ANN-ih-mul] (stress on first, clear l at end)[a-ni-MAL] (clear consonantal l)

papel[pa-PEU][pah-PEL][pa-PEL]
civil[si-VIU][SIV-ill][si-VIL]

Polish ear test: does the final sound rhyme with łapa's ł or with English bell's l? It should rhyme with łapa.

2. -de and -te palatalization (BP [dʒi] / [tʃi], not EP [d] / [t])

In BP, final -de and -te palatalize: de becomes the sound of Polish dżi (English "j" + "ee"), and te becomes Polish czi (English "ch" + "ee"). EP keeps a hard d or t, often with the final vowel almost silent.

cidade[si-DA-dʒi] (Polish: "si-DA-dżi")[si-DAD] (final almost silent)
gente[ZHEN-tʃi] (Polish: "żen-czi")[ZHEN-t]
dente[DEN-tʃi][DEN-t]
ponte[PON-tʃi][PON-t]

Polish ear test: does cidade end with the same sound as Polish dżin? That's BP. If it ends with a hard d or trails off silently, that's EP.

3. Initial r and double rr (BP [h], not EP [ʁ])

BP initial r and double rr sound like the English h in "hello" or the German ch in "Bach" (a back fricative). EP and French use a uvular [ʁ] (the throaty French r), which is qualitatively different.

WordBP targetEP / French drift
rua[HU-a] (English "hoo-ah") or [CHU-a] (German "Bach"-style)[ʁU-a] (French throaty r)
carro[CA-hu][CA-ʁu]
terra[TE-ha][TE-ʁa]
real[he-AU][ʁe-AL]

German ear test: does the initial r sound like German Bach's ch, or like French Paris's r? BP wants the German-style sound.

4. Coda s (BP [s] / [z], not EP [ʃ] / [ʒ])

When s ends a syllable in BP, it stays as English [s] or [z]. In EP, it becomes Polish sz or English sh.

WordBP targetEP drift
festa[FES-ta] (clear "s")[FESH-ta] (Polish "sz")
escola[es-KO-la][esh-KO-la]
três[TRES][TRESH]
isto[IS-tu][ISH-tu]

Polish ear test: does the coda s sound like Polish s (in samolot), or like Polish sz (in szafa)? BP wants the plain s.

5. Final unstressed -e (BP [i], not EP silent)

BP renders unstressed final -e as a clear [i] (Polish i). EP often reduces it to a centralized [ɨ] (closer to Polish y) or drops it entirely.

WordBP targetEP drift
nome[NO-mi] (Polish "no-mi")[NOM] or [NO-mə] (silent / muffled)
fome[FO-mi][FOM]
sete[SE-tʃi] (combines with #2)[SET]

Polish ear test: does the word end on a clear i sound? BP yes; EP no.

6. Stress placement on cognates (BP often final, English often first)

Same-spelled cognates almost always have different stress in BP vs English.

WordBP stressEnglish stress
animala-ni-MAU (final)AN-ih-mul (initial)

socialso-si-AU (final)SO-shul (initial)
totalto-TAU (final)TO-tul (initial)

Quick test: if the heaviest syllable is the FIRST one and the word is spelled the same in English, suspect English drift.

Special signals you may notice

  • h- initial words: BP keeps h silent (hora = [O-ra], hotel

= [o-TEU]). English aspirates it. If you hear a clear English h-, it's EN drift.

  • English aspiration on initial stops: t-, p-, k- in English

carry a puff of air. BP doesn't. Subtle, but if a word feels "explosive" on the initial consonant, that's EN.

  • Voice consistency: in calibration, ≥1 clip per voice should be

represented. If a voice systematically lands MISPRONOUNCED across many unrelated words, that's a voice-level problem (high-risk voice), not a per-word problem. Mark them all and the gate logic handles it.

How to use the radio buttons

Stage 8 calibration (08_2): OK / MISPRONOUNCED / UNCLEAR

  • OK — sounds clearly like Brazilian Portuguese. None of the six

features above are noticeably off. Doesn't have to be perfect, just clearly BP and not another language.

  • MISPRONOUNCED — clearly off. Final l sounds English. -de is

silent (EP). Initial r is French-throaty. Stress is on the wrong syllable. Anything that an ear-test above lights up.

  • UNCLEAR — borderline; you can't decide; it sounds weird but you

can't pinpoint why. Use this freely — unclear rows route to a small manual queue, they don't break the pipeline.

Stage 8 after-fix (08_6): BETTER / SAME / WORSE

You'll hear two clips per row: the original (v1) and the fixed (v2). Both are the same word, same voice. The alias-respelling pipeline tried to fix the v1 mispronunciation.

  • BETTER — v2 sounds clearly more BP than v1. The fix worked.
  • SAME — v2 sounds the same as v1. The alias didn't actually change

anything audible. This blocks the rebuild and triggers a stronger respelling.

  • WORSE — v2 sounds worse than v1 (alias introduced a new artifact —

e.g., a weird hiatus, a wrong vowel). Hard-stop signal.

Pacing tips

  • Don't agonize. ~30–40 minutes for the full calibration sample is

about right. Spend ~20 seconds per clip on average.

  • Trust your first impression. If a clip sounds wrong on first listen,

it's MISPRONOUNCED.

  • If you replay a clip more than three times trying to decide, it's

UNCLEAR.

  • Calibration is sampling, not exhaustive judgment. The pipeline expands

the sample automatically if a family shows >10% MISPRONOUNCED.

EP red-flag words you may notice

These are known EP-leaning entries from earlier audits — if you hear them rendered EP, that's a true positive:

camisola, troço, vosso, comboio, equipa, registo, utilizador, paragem, desporto, golo, aquando, decerto, susceptível (BP would normally be suscetível), assente, proveniente.
